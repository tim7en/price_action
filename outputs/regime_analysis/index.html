1 · The market through the regimes

The broad market (Wilshire) is shown on a log scale, with the background shaded by the growth/inflation quadrant and a ribbon underneath marking the corporate debt-cycle phase. The cap-weighted composite built from the 257 companies' own prices, and the S&P 500, are overlaid for cross-checking. The composite is survivorship-biased (only today's constituents) so it overstates long-run returns; treat the Wilshire line as the honest benchmark.

2 · The corporate debt cycle

Aggregating every company into one “corporate sector”, this is Dalio's debt lens applied bottom-up: how much leverage the corporate sector carries (debt / EBITDA), whether it can service that debt (interest coverage), and whether profits and cash flow are expanding or contracting. Leverage that rises for years and then rolls over is the classic setup before a de-leveraging.

3 · Beautiful vs. ugly deleveraging

Dalio's central distinction: a de-leveraging is beautiful when debt falls relative to income while the economy keeps growing (debt/income down, nominal growth positive, credit conditions calm), and ugly / deflationary when debt only falls through defaults and contraction. Here we track corporate leverage against nominal revenue growth, with the phase shaded behind.

4 · The quadrant map, paid by forward returns

Every month plotted by its growth momentum (x) and inflation momentum (y), colored by the equity return over the next 12 months. Green clusters show which corner of the machine has historically rewarded risk; the star is today.

For each policy / financial-condition / capital-allocation driver, we correlate its monthly change with the equity return over the following 1–24 months. The ringed cell is the horizon of peak effect — our estimate of “how long before we see it”. Red = a rise in the driver is followed by weaker returns; blue = stronger.

7 · Predicting the next regime

Turning the regime history into a Markov chain gives the monthly probability of moving from one regime to another (left), and propagating today's state forward gives the distribution of where we are likely to be in 12 months (right). Transitions are Laplace-smoothed so a recent regime does not falsely look permanent.

Method & caveats

  • Data. Macro from the local FRED/daily store (1999–present); corporate aggregates summed across 239 companies' quarterly filings, trailing-twelve-month.
  • Survivorship. The fundamentals set is today's constituents, so the composite index and early aggregate ratios are upward-biased. The Wilshire index is used as the unbiased market benchmark.
  • Reporting breadth. Aggregate ratios use only quarters with ≥110 reporting companies; early-2000s coverage is thin.
  • Regimes are descriptive, not causal. Lead-lag correlations are associations; the peak-lag horizons are robust in sign but should not be traded mechanically.
